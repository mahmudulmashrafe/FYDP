\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Migraine is a chronic, debilitating neurovascular disorder affecting approximately 15\% of the global population, characterized by cortical hyperexcitability, abnormal sensory gating, and thalamocortical dysrhythmia (TCD). Conventional pharmacological treatments often entail adverse side effects, medication overuse headaches, or incomplete efficacy. This Final Year Design Project introduces an end-to-end neuroengineering framework integrating electroencephalography (EEG) time-frequency computer vision classification with personalized acoustic neuromodulation for non-invasive, drug-free migraine management. 

Using the Carnegie Mellon University (CMU) ultra-high-density EEG repository recorded across 128 scalp channels during resting-state and sensory stimulation (auditory SSAEP and visual SSVEP), we established a standardized preprocessing pipeline incorporating common average referencing (CAR), 1.0--50.0~Hz bandpass filtering, and 60~Hz notch filtering. Occipital channel \#80 recordings were transformed via Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) into calibrated time-frequency spectrogram images rendered with the Viridis colormap across a $[-10, +30]$~dB dynamic range. For interictal migraine detection, deep convolutional transfer learning was evaluated across three distinct architectures: VGG16, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB7. EfficientNetB7 demonstrated superior diagnostic capability, achieving 93.75\% accuracy, 94.71\% weighted precision, 93.85\% weighted F1-score, and a Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of 0.8750, followed by VGG16 (90.63\% accuracy, 0.7985 MCC) and ResNet50 (87.50\% accuracy, 0.7410 MCC).

To bridge automated detection with non-invasive intervention, an analytical colormap inversion engine was formulated. By projecting pixel RGB vectors onto a precomputed 256-color Viridis Look-Up Table (LUT) via Euclidean distance minimization, calibrated spectral power ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$) was accurately reconstructed directly from spectrogram images. This enabled the quantification of individual electrophysiological rhythm deficits, notably the Theta-to-Alpha Ratio (TAR) and the patient's exact Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF). Population cohort analytics across resting-state subjects revealed an elevated mean theta proportion (15.47\% in migraine vs. 13.17\% in controls) and marked alpha suppression. Using the patient-specific IAF, a digital audio synthesis engine generated customized dichotic pure-tone binaural beat prescriptions centered at a 216~Hz carrier frequency with 1.5-second squared half-cosine taper envelopes to prevent acoustic switching transients. For an exemplar migraine patient (M10), the system localized an IAF peak at 10.09~Hz, identified an alpha power deficit of 7.88\%, and synthesized an exact 10.09~Hz beat prescription ($f_{\text{left}} = 210.96$~Hz, $f_{\text{right}} = 221.05$~Hz) formatted as 16-bit 44.1~kHz stereo audio. This work establishes a closed-loop technological bridge between deep learning-based neural diagnostics and personalized auditory cortical entrainment, offering a scalable, reproducible digital therapeutic paradigm for migraine mitigation.

\section{Project Overview}
\label{sec:project_overview}
Migraine is an intensely debilitating chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of moderate-to-severe throbbing headache, typically unilateral, often accompanied by autonomic, cognitive, and sensory disturbances including nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia \cite{Puledda2024,Ashina2023}. In approximately one-third of sufferers, the headache is preceded or accompanied by transient focal neurological symptoms termed migraine aura \cite{Riederer2023,Puledda2024}. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies, migraine ranks among the top three causes of disability worldwide, affecting over 1.1 billion individuals and representing the leading cause of years lived with disability (YLD) among young and middle-aged adults, particularly women \cite{Stovner2022,GBD2023,Jiang2025}.

Despite the immense individual and socioeconomic burden of migraine, clinical management remains heavily reliant on acute and preventive pharmacotherapy. Common pharmacological agents include triptans, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), beta-blockers, topiramate, and recently developed calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor antagonists \cite{Amiri2022,Puledda2024}. However, clinical studies report that a substantial proportion of patients experience inadequate relief, severe adverse side effects, contraindications, or treatment refractoriness \cite{Shamsi2024,Ashina2023}. Furthermore, frequent intake of acute analgesics often triggers medication overuse headache (MOH), transforming episodic migraine into a intractable chronic state \cite{GomezPilar2022}. Consequently, there is an urgent and growing clinical demand for non-invasive, non-pharmacological digital therapeutics capable of objective detection and patient-tailored intervention.

Recent advances in quantitative electroencephalography (qEEG) and high-density neuroimaging have revealed that migraine is characterized by profound neurophysiological abnormalities even during interictal (attack-free) intervals \cite{VanDenHoek2024,Coppola2023}. Chief among these signatures is \textit{thalamocortical dysrhythmia} (TCD) and pervasive cortical hyperexcitability, manifested as altered low-frequency oscillatory power, impaired habituation to repetitive sensory stimuli, disrupted interhemispheric coherence, and marked suppression of posterior alpha rhythms (8.0--13.0~Hz) \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,Zhang2023,FrimpongManson2024}. Concurrently, non-invasive auditory neuromodulation via \textit{binaural beats} has emerged as an effective modality for driving targeted brain rhythms through acoustic frequency-following responses (FFR) in subcortical and cortical auditory pathways \cite{Oster1973,Wahbeh2007,Ingendoh2023}.

However, existing approaches in literature remain fragmented. Conventional EEG diagnostic pipelines focus almost exclusively on offline classification without providing therapeutic intervention pathways \cite{aslan2023migrainecnn,orhanbulucu2023hybrid}. Conversely, existing commercial binaural beat applications deliver generic, pre-recorded audio tracks with arbitrary fixed beat frequencies (e.g., standard 10~Hz) that fail to account for the patient's unique neurophysiological state, individual alpha frequency (IAF), or real-time spectral power deficits \cite{CoronaGonzalez2021,KahathuduwaBlume2025}. This lack of personalization significantly limits therapeutic efficacy.

This Final Year Design Project bridges this fundamental gap by proposing an integrated, closed-loop wearable EEG framework for real-time interictal migraine detection and personalized mitigation via binaural beat entrainment. The core architecture leverages ultra-high-density EEG recordings, transforms multichannel signals into high-resolution Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) time-frequency spectrograms, applies deep transfer learning with state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks (VGG16, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB7) for automated classification, and introduces an analytical RGB colormap inversion algorithm that extracts patient-specific Individual Alpha Frequencies directly from spectrograms to synthesize customized dichotic binaural beat audio prescriptions.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
The motivation for this project stems from both urgent clinical needs and emerging computational engineering opportunities:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Need for Non-Pharmacological Interventions}: Over 40\% of migraine patients discontinue pharmaceutical prophylaxis due to tolerability issues, cognitive dulling, weight changes, or rebound headaches \cite{Amiri2022,Shamsi2024}. A non-invasive digital therapeutic that restores physiological brainwave balance without chemical toxicity offers a paradigm shift in chronic migraine care.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Objectivity via Biomarkers}: Migraine diagnosis currently relies on subjective patient recall and clinical symptom checklists (ICHD-3 criteria), which are susceptible to diagnostic delay and recall bias \cite{Petrusic2025}. Quantitative EEG biomarkers provide objective, reproducible neurophysiological evidence of cortical hyperexcitability and sensory processing dysfunction \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,VanDenHoek2024}.
    \item \textbf{Computer Vision on Time-Frequency Representations}: Raw 1D EEG voltage traces are noisy, non-stationary, and prone to subject-level impedance variations. Converting multichannel EEG into 2D time-frequency spectrograms preserves temporal dynamics alongside spectral harmonics, enabling the deployment of powerful computer vision models pre-trained on massive image corpora \cite{orhanbulucu2023hybrid,bravoortiz2024spectrocvt,wei2025densenet}.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic Precision via Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF)}: Canonical alpha band definitions (8.0--13.0~Hz) overlook inter-individual neurobiological diversity; an individual's intrinsic dominant alpha peak varies significantly with age, gender, and cortical pathology \cite{CoronaGonzalez2021,KahathuduwaBlume2025}. Acoustic entrainment tuned precisely to the patient's measured IAF maximizes thalamocortical resonance and auditory-cortex phase locking, overcoming the limitations of fixed-frequency commercial audio \cite{Melnichuk2025,Hamid2025}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
The concrete engineering and scientific objectives of this research project are defined as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To implement a standardized biomedical signal processing pipeline for ultra-high-density (128-channel) EEG data, performing channel verification, common average referencing (CAR), bandpass filtering (1.0--50.0~Hz), and 60~Hz powerline notch filtering.
    \item To generate calibrated 2D time-frequency STFT spectrogram images from visual/occipital channel recordings across resting-state and sensory-evoked (auditory SSAEP and visual SSVEP) paradigms.
    \item To design, train, and evaluate deep convolutional transfer learning architectures (VGG16, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB7) for automated interictal migraine detection, benchmarking performance via accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).
    \item To formulate and validate an analytical RGB colormap inversion algorithm capable of converting pixel-level Viridis spectrogram images back into calibrated decibel and linear power spectral density ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$) profiles.
    \item To accurately localize patient-specific Individual Alpha Frequencies (IAF) and compute quantitative electrophysiological indices, including the Theta-to-Alpha Ratio (TAR) and Alpha Power Deficits.
    \item To engineer a high-fidelity digital audio synthesis engine generating calibrated, click-free stereo binaural beat audio prescriptions modulated exactly to the patient's IAF with smooth cosine fade envelopes.
    \item To conduct cohort-level statistical analytics across migraine and control populations and evaluate the system within realistic engineering, ethical, and acoustic safety constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:methodology}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1: Signal Preprocessing and Montage Standardisation}: Continuous raw EEG recordings acquired in BioSemi BDF format from the CMU ultra-high-density dataset are ingested using MNE-Python. Non-scalp channels are excluded, common average referencing is applied across 128 scalp electrodes, and zero-phase FIR filtering isolates 1.0--50.0~Hz activity while rejecting 60~Hz line interference. Channel \#80, located over the visual/occipital cortex, is selected due to its primary role in photophobia and migraine cortical hyperexcitability \cite{chamanzar2021uhd}.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2: Time-Frequency Spectrogram Generation}: Signals are decomposed via Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with Hann windowing into time-frequency power representations spanning 1.0 to 50.0~Hz. These representations are rendered into high-resolution PNG images using Matplotlib's Viridis colormap across a calibrated $[-10, +30]$~dB display range.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3: Deep Transfer Learning Classification}: Spectrogram images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels, normalized, and partitioned into training and testing sets. Three distinct CNN architectures---VGG16 \cite{Simonyan2014}, ResNet50 \cite{He2016}, and EfficientNetB7 \cite{Tan2019}---are instantiated with ImageNet pre-trained weights. A customized dense classification head with global average pooling, ReLU activation, dropout, and a two-neuron softmax layer is optimized using Adam ($\eta = 10^{-4}$) and Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy loss.
    \item \textbf{Stage 4: Mathematical Spectrogram Inversion}: To extract quantitative physiological indices from rendered images without requiring multi-gigabyte raw EEG arrays, an exact RGB lookup table (LUT) inversion algorithm is deployed. By computing the minimum Euclidean distance in sRGB color space between image pixels and a 256-color Viridis LUT, pixel intensities are translated into decibels and linear power spectral density ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$). Frequency rows are mapped from 50.0~Hz down to 1.0~Hz.
    \item \textbf{Stage 5: Biomarker Quantification and Audio Prescription Synthesis}: From the reconstructed PSD, canonical band powers (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma) are integrated. The Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF) is detected via peak search within 8.0--13.0~Hz, and the Theta-to-Alpha Ratio (TAR) is computed. Using the patient's IAF as the beat difference ($f_{\text{beat}}$) and a soothing carrier frequency ($f_c = 216.0$~Hz), dichotic pure sine waves ($f_{\text{left}} = f_c - f_{\text{beat}}/2$; $f_{\text{right}} = f_c + f_{\text{beat}}/2$) are synthesized with squared half-cosine onset and offset tapers to generate click-free, 16-bit 44.1~kHz stereo audio files.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

\subsection{Neurovascular Pathophysiology of Migraine}
Migraine is increasingly recognized not merely as a vascular headache, but as a complex, familial neurovascular disorder characterized by widespread dysregulation of subcortical and cortical neural networks \cite{Puledda2024,Ashina2023}. At the physiological core of migraine susceptibility is \textit{cortical hyperexcitability} and a profound failure of habituation \cite{Coppola2023,VanDenHoek2024}. In healthy neural circuits, repetitive sensory stimulation (e.g., visual strobe flashes or auditory tone bursts) elicits initial cortical evoked potentials that progressively decrease in amplitude---a protective physiological process known as sensory gating or habituation \cite{Coppola2023}. In migraine patients, this habituation is deficient or absent during the interictal period, leading to cortical sensory overload, photophobia, and phonophobia \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,AbdulhusseinAldeen2024}.

Underlying this habituation deficit is \textit{thalamocortical dysrhythmia} (TCD) \cite{chamanzar2021uhd}. TCD occurs when abnormal hyperpolarization of thalamic relay neurons de-inactivates low-threshold T-type calcium channels, causing thalamic neurons to shift from tonic firing into rhythmic burst firing in the low-frequency theta range (4.0--8.0~Hz). This aberrant low-frequency thalamic pacemaking disrupts normal corticothalamic feedback loops, producing a characteristic surrounding halo of high-frequency beta and gamma activation (the ``edge effect'') alongside a marked suppression of normal posterior alpha oscillations (8.0--13.0~Hz) \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,Coppola2023}. Restoring alpha rhythm synchronization is therefore hypothesized to normalize thalamocortical gating and attenuate sensory hyperexcitability.

\subsection{EEG Frequency Bands and Their Clinical Significance}
Electroencephalography measures post-synaptic dendritic potentials from large populations of pyramidal cortical neurons. In clinical neurophysiology, scalp EEG is decomposed into five canonical frequency bands:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delta ($\boldsymbol{\delta}$, 1.0--4.0~Hz)}: High-amplitude slow waves dominant during deep slow-wave sleep (NREM Stage 3/4). Pathological focal delta in awake adults reflects cortical de-afferentation, structural lesions, or severe post-ictal slowing \cite{Fan2024,Dabiri2022}.
    \item \textbf{Theta ($\boldsymbol{\theta}$, 4.0--8.0~Hz)}: Associated with drowsiness, memory consolidation, and hypnagogic imagery. In migraine, elevated resting theta power is a direct electrophysiological hallmark of thalamocortical dysrhythmia \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,Zebhauser2024}.
    \item \textbf{Alpha ($\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, 8.0--13.0~Hz)}: Prominent over posterior occipital and parietal regions during relaxed, awake states with eyes closed. Alpha represents an active sensory inhibition gating mechanism. Suppression of alpha power or shifting of the Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF) correlates directly with central pain sensitization, cognitive stress, and migraine vulnerability \cite{FrimpongManson2024,Hamid2025}.
    \item \textbf{Beta ($\boldsymbol{\beta}$, 13.0--30.0~Hz)}: Low-amplitude, high-frequency oscillations linked to active cognitive alertness, motor planning, and anxious arousal. Elevated high-beta is widely observed in interictal migraineurs and chronic headache sufferers \cite{Kim2023,Melnichuk2025}.
    \item \textbf{Gamma ($\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, 30.0--50.0~Hz)}: Associated with feature binding, focal attention, and multi-sensory integration. In migraine, abnormal gamma synchronization reflects visual cortical hyper-reactivity \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,WangZhang2022}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Biophysics and Physiology of Binaural Beat Entrainment}
Binaural beats are an auditory perceptual illusion discovered by Heinrich Wilhelm Dove in 1839 and extensively characterized in human neurophysiology by Gerald Oster in 1973 \cite{Oster1973}. When two coherent pure sinusoidal acoustic tones of slightly differing frequencies, $f_1$ and $f_2$, are presented dichotically (one tone into each ear via stereo headphones), the acoustic waves do not mix physically in the air. Instead, neural signals from the cochleae travel along the auditory nerve to the \textit{superior olivary complex} (SOC) in the brainstem---the primary anatomical site for binaural sound localization and interaural phase disparity detection \cite{Oster1973,Wahbeh2007}.

The medial superior olive (MSO) neurons act as coincidence detectors, measuring microsecond-level phase differences between the two ears. This neural phase comparison generates an amplitude-modulated neural discharge at the exact arithmetic difference frequency:
\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|
    \label{eq:binaural_beat_diff}
\end{equation}
This beat frequency is propagated rostrally via the lateral lemniscus and inferior colliculus to the medial geniculate body of the thalamus and auditory cortex, eliciting a cortical \textit{Frequency-Following Response} (FFR) \cite{Oster1973,Wahbeh2007,Melnichuk2025}. For binaural beats to be perceived, two biophysical conditions must be met:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The carrier frequency $f_c = (f_1 + f_2)/2$ must lie below approximately 1000~Hz (with optimal phase-locking occurring between 200~Hz and 500~Hz), as auditory nerve fibers lose phase-locking capacity above 1.5~kHz \cite{Oster1973,Wahbeh2007}.
    \item The beat frequency $f_{\text{beat}}$ must be less than approximately 30--35~Hz; larger frequency separations are perceived as two distinct musical tones rather than a fused pulsating beat \cite{Oster1973}.
\end{enumerate}
By tuning $f_{\text{beat}}$ precisely to the alpha band (8.0--13.0~Hz), binaural beats promote thalamocortical synchronization, enhance parasympathetic vagal tone, and suppress stress-induced cortical hyper-arousal \cite{Wahbeh2007,KahathuduwaBlume2025,Hamid2025}.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}
In accordance with recent methodological syntheses in neuroengineering and digital therapeutics, the literature is categorized into four interconnected domains: (1) EEG and biomarker-related studies in migraine, (2) binaural beats and acoustic entrainment, (3) wearable EEG systems and digital phenotyping, and (4) time-frequency spectrogram feature extraction and deep learning.

\subsection{EEG and Biomarker-Related Studies in Migraine}
For decades, researchers have investigated electrophysiological alterations as objective biomarkers of the migraine brain state. Van den Hoek et al.~\cite{VanDenHoek2024} presented a comprehensive review of spontaneous resting-state EEG, evoked potentials, and sensory reactivity across the migraine cycle. They concluded that preictal and interictal phases exhibit consistent alterations in theta and alpha power, cortical slowing, and functional connectivity deficits, though methodological heterogeneity across past studies has hindered clinical translation.

Kim et al.~\cite{Kim2023} conducted a large-scale quantitative EEG (qEEG) study involving 720 migraine patients utilizing normative z-score topographic maps. Their findings demonstrated that individuals with migraine exhibit significantly elevated absolute power across all frequency bands compared to healthy controls, with the most robust diagnostic distinction occurring in high-frequency beta (25--30~Hz) and altered alpha distribution, confirming qEEG's diagnostic viability.

Examining computational models for stress and neurological states, Joshi et al.~\cite{JoshiMatharu2025} developed spiking neural networks (SNNs) and convolutional spiking neural networks (CSNNs) for temporal EEG analysis, achieving 98.61\% accuracy with minimal energy consumption, emphasizing the feasibility of lightweight edge models for wearable health devices. In an investigation of objective migraine markers across prospective and retrospective cohorts, Frimpong-Manson, Araujo, et al.~\cite{FrimpongManson2024,araujo2024eegsignatures} identified interictal increased occipital 12--13~Hz power as a highly reproducible biomarker, achieving 87.2\% classification accuracy with K-nearest neighbors (KNN) and support vector machines (SVM).

Zebhauser et al.~\cite{Zebhauser2024} performed a meta-analysis of 27 resting-state EEG and MEG studies, confirming a candidate neurophysiological signature comprising increased low-frequency theta power, abnormal occipital alpha oscillations, and diminished alpha-to-beta long-range functional connectivity. Abdulhussein et al.~\cite{AbdulhusseinAldeen2024} utilized high-density EEG and Steady-State Visual Evoked Potentials (SSVEPs) at 4~Hz and 6~Hz, reporting that migraineurs display significantly elevated cortical coherence in occipital-parietal networks, consistent with thalamocortical dysrhythmia.

In clinical differentiation studies, Riederer et al.~\cite{Riederer2023} analyzed EEG and MRI signatures of migraine with aura, noting that while visual scotomas often preserve scalp EEG rhythms, complex prolonged auras produce focal acute-phase slowing that differentiates migraine from epileptic spikes or ischemic strokes. Göker~\cite{Goker2023} proposed an automated migraine detection framework using Welch PSD estimation (1--49~Hz) combined with a 128-channel Bidirectional LSTM network, achieving 95.99\% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.96. Kedgan et al.~\cite{Kedgan2025} performed a systematic pooled prevalence meta-analysis across phases of hemiplegic migraine, demonstrating distinct stage-dependent electrophysiological patterns from contralateral background suppression during aura to generalized slowing during headache.

Furthermore, Sebastianelli et al.~\cite{sebastianelli2025ann} applied artificial neural networks (ANNs) to somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) in 177 subjects (91 episodic migraineurs, 86 controls), achieving 72.8\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.79. Hsiao et al.~\cite{hsiao2024brainstem} examined somatosensory evoked EEG in 342 participants using spectral Granger causality and SVMs, proving that abnormal brainstem-cortical gating interactions differentiate chronic migraineurs from healthy controls ($p < 0.001$).

\subsection{Binaural Beats and Auditory Neuromodulation}
The therapeutic application of acoustic brainwave entrainment has expanded significantly in recent years. Melnichuk et al.~\cite{Melnichuk2025} conducted parametric investigations of beta (16~Hz) and gamma (40~Hz) binaural beats during sustained attention Continuous Performance Tasks (CPT), demonstrating robust EEG entrainment and vigilance stabilization under optimal carrier conditions (340~Hz carrier with background pink noise).

Yang et al.~\cite{Yang2025} executed a randomized crossover trial evaluating 6~Hz, 10~Hz, and 25~Hz binaural beats in healthy subjects, reporting that all frequencies significantly lowered resting heart rate and arterial blood pressure, with alpha beats (10~Hz) driving the strongest parasympathetic autonomic shift (increased high-frequency heart rate variability, HF-HRV). In a meta-analysis of 15 randomized controlled trials, Xiong et al.~\cite{Xiong2025} demonstrated that binaural beat stimulation delivers substantial reductions in perioperative anxiety (Standardized Mean Difference, SMD: $-1.0$ to $-1.4$) alongside significant analgesia.

Crucially, Kahathuduwa et al.~\cite{KahathuduwaBlume2025} pioneered closed-loop, real-time EEG-guided binaural beat stimulation, demonstrating that dynamically adjusting acoustic beat frequencies based on instantaneous scalp EEG recordings produced superior relaxation and cognitive performance compared to open-loop, static stimulation. Shamsi et al.~\cite{Shamsi2024} reviewed clinical pain interventions, finding that theta and alpha binaural beats significantly attenuate acute procedural pain, although studies on chronic headache remain constrained by small cohorts and non-personalized stimulus parameters.

Sudre et al.~\cite{Sudre2024} utilized wearable EEG to compare stationary binaural beats against spatially panning dynamic acoustic stimuli, finding that spatially modulated beats induced stronger subjective relaxation and elevated posterior alpha power. Fan et al.~\cite{Fan2024} established that low-frequency beats at 0.25~Hz significantly shortened sleep-onset latency to slow-wave sleep during daytime naps. Mosheshe et al.~\cite{Mosheshe2024} proved that binaural beats mitigated acute physiological stress and state anxiety during high-stress medical cadaveric dissection.

Dose-response dynamics were analyzed by Chockboondee et al.~\cite{ChockboondeeJatupornpoonsub2023}, who demonstrated that sessions exceeding 20 minutes produced progressive reductions in sympathetic autonomic arousal. Dabiri et al.~\cite{Dabiri2022} showed that delta binaural beats improved subjective sleep quality and mood among commercial airline pilots. Most importantly for our project, Corona-González et al.~\cite{CoronaGonzalez2021} demonstrated that \textit{customized} binaural beats tuned to each subject's individual EEG peak frequency enhanced target band power significantly more than fixed, standard frequencies, directly validating our personalized IAF-tuning strategy.

In neurocognitive tasks, Al-Shargie et al.~\cite{AlShargie2022} combined fNIRS with 16~Hz beats during cognitive Stroop tasks, observing a 44\% reduction in salivary alpha-amylase and enhanced prefrontal cortical oxygenation. Wang et al.~\cite{WangZhang2022} verified that 40~Hz gamma beats elevated frontal alpha/gamma power and improved working memory capacity. Finally, Hamid et al.~\cite{Hamid2025} compared alpha binaural beats directly against white noise, proving via 64-channel EEG that alpha beats elicit distinct phase-synchronized cortical networks in parietal-occipital areas that enhance cognitive gating.

\subsection{Wearable EEG Systems and Digital Phenotyping}
The transition of neural monitoring from shielded clinical laboratories to wearable consumer form-factors is transforming chronic disease management. Petru\v{s}i\'{c}~\cite{Petrusic2025} reviewed digital phenotyping for migraine, defining wearable brain--computer interfaces (BCIs) as a scalable, non-invasive frontier capable of continuous neural state tracking under free-living conditions. Petru\v{s}i\'{c} categorized wearable architectures into scalp-EEG, forehead-EEG, and in-ear-EEG, noting that while scalp montages provide superior cortical resolution (achieving 90--96\% classification accuracy), practical deployment requires addressing motion artifacts, electrode impedance instability, and user compliance.

He et al.~\cite{He2023} conducted a comprehensive survey of commercial wireless EEG platforms, analyzing variations across channel count (from 1 to 64 channels), sampling rates (128--1000~Hz), battery autonomy, and pricing (\$250 to \$30,000). They emphasized that widespread clinical translation is bottlenecked by the absence of standardized signal validation pipelines and automated real-time artifact rejection. Addressing affordability, Muhammad et al.~\cite{Muhammad2023} developed an ultra-low-cost, 16-channel OpenBCI-compatible headset achieving 82.9\% classification accuracy with an assembly time under 3 minutes at a total cost of \$299, demonstrating that a reduced 3-channel occipital configuration retained 82.0\% diagnostic sensitivity.

Danelakis et al.~\cite{DanelakisStubberud2025} surveyed artificial intelligence and wearable sensors across primary headache disorders, reporting that machine learning models achieve diagnostic accuracies between 62\% and 99.7\%. However, they cautioned that existing literature is plagued by small sample sizes, lack of subject-independent validation, and severe risk of overfitting. Van Der Donckt et al.~\cite{VanDerDonckt2025} analyzed daytime movement patterns in migraineurs using wearable accelerometers, revealing statistically significant reductions in physical activity during pre-ictal and ictal states ($p < 0.001$), supporting the integration of multi-sensor digital phenotyping with wearable EEG.

\subsection{Time-Frequency Spectrogram Feature Extraction and Deep Learning}
Rather than relying on manually engineered 1D statistical features (variance, skewness, Hjorth parameters) that discard critical non-stationary dynamics, converting multichannel EEG into 2D time-frequency image representations (spectrograms and scalograms) has emerged as the premier paradigm in biomedical signal processing. Chamanzar, Haigh, Grover, and Behrmann~\cite{chamanzar2021uhd} established the foundational CMU Ultra-High-Density EEG repository (128 channels) used in this project, demonstrating that interictal migraineurs display abnormal cortical coherence and phase locking during visual and auditory steady-state stimulation.

Directly building upon this CMU dataset, Orhanbulucu, Latifoglu, and Baydemir~\cite{orhanbulucu2023hybrid} compared Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) spectrograms against Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) scalograms. Feeding these images into transfer-learned AlexNet, ResNet50, and SqueezeNet backbones, they achieved 94.7\% accuracy, proving that 2D time-frequency representations of channel \#80 reliably capture migraine-specific cortical hyperexcitability across sensory and resting conditions.

In parallel, Aslan~\cite{aslan2023migrainecnn} utilized CWT scalograms from the same CMU dataset, designing a custom deep CNN and benchmarking it against VGG16, DenseNet121, ResNet101, and Xception. Aslan reported up to 100\% classification accuracy and utilized Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) to demonstrate that the networks focused specifically on altered occipital alpha and beta harmonics.

Beyond the CMU dataset, Alsabea et al.~\cite{alsabea2024migraine} achieved 97\% classification accuracy on clinical migraine EEG using bandpass filtering, ICA, and 2D Power Spectral Density features with CNNs. Yuvaraj et al.~\cite{Yuvaraj2023} evaluated statistical, fractal dimension, and wavelet features, demonstrating that non-linear time-frequency representations significantly outperform static time-domain statistics. Kalashami et al.~\cite{Kalashami2022} and Luo et al.~\cite{Luo2024} established that deep learning models ingesting wavelet packet decompositions and time-frequency images provide robust cross-subject generalization in affective and neurological decoding.

The broader efficacy of transfer learning on EEG spectrograms is demonstrated across adjacent neurological conditions. Bravo-Ortiz et al.~\cite{bravoortiz2024spectrocvt} introduced SpectroCVT-Net, combining convolutional vision transformers with channel attention on EEG spectrograms for Alzheimer's disease diagnosis. Wei and Mooney~\cite{wei2025densenet} deployed DenseNet backbones on spectrograms to classify general EEG abnormalities. Dursun et al.~\cite{dursun2025multilayer} demonstrated multi-layer neural network detection of migraine, epilepsy, and schizophrenia from spectral representations. Pattnaik et al.~\cite{pattnaik2024scalogram}, Edderbali et al.~\cite{edderbali2024epilepsy}, and Khan et al.~\cite{khan2024seizurespectrogram} utilized pre-trained CNNs on scalograms and spectrograms for automated epileptic seizure detection, confirming that ImageNet-pretrained weights transfer seamlessly to time-frequency representations. Similar successes have been reported by Al-Qazzaz et al.~\cite{alqazzaz2024autism} for autism spectrum disorder, Amer and Belhaouari~\cite{amer2024neuroscience} for neurodegenerative disease screening, and Yousufi et al.~\cite{yousufi2024multimodal} for depression recognition via multimodal EEG-audio spectrogram fusion.

\section{Research Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:gap_analysis}
A systematic synthesis of the literature reveals four fundamental research gaps that motivate the architecture of this Final Year Design Project:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Gap (Biomarker Inconsistency and 1D Modeling)}: Existing clinical studies rely on disparate, univariate EEG metrics (e.g., resting alpha power alone or isolated ERP latencies) that exhibit high inter-subject variance and fail to capture multi-scale temporal-spectral interactions \cite{VanDenHoek2024,Zebhauser2024}. Furthermore, conventional machine learning models relying on handcrafted 1D features discard critical phase and harmonic structures present across time \cite{RaghuramanJoshi2024}. Our work addresses this by utilizing full 2D STFT spectrogram images spanning 1.0--50.0~Hz and deploying modern transfer-learned deep convolutional backbones (VGG16, ResNet50, EfficientNetB7).
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic Gap (Lack of Acoustic Personalization)}: Despite extensive evidence that the human alpha rhythm exhibits significant inter-individual frequency shifts (IAF typically varying from 8.5 to 11.5~Hz in healthy adults, and severely depressed in migraineurs) \cite{CoronaGonzalez2021,KahathuduwaBlume2025}, commercial binaural beat applications utilize arbitrary, fixed frequencies (e.g., standard 10.0~Hz). Fixed stimulation fails to elicit maximal thalamocortical resonance and can cause cognitive fatigue. Our framework closes this gap by computing the patient's exact IAF from the empirical power spectrum and modulating the binaural beat frequency to match individual neurophysiology.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Disconnection (Separation of Diagnosis and Therapy)}: In literature, diagnostic classification and acoustic entrainment exist as isolated research tracks. Machine learning researchers publish classification models without therapeutic pathways \cite{aslan2023migrainecnn,orhanbulucu2023hybrid}, while neuromodulation researchers conduct clinical trials using manual, uncalibrated audio generators \cite{Xiong2025,Shamsi2024}. No prior framework integrates automated deep learning detection with automated, patient-tailored audio synthesis.
    \item \textbf{Data Ingestion Bottleneck (Storage and Multi-Channel Overhead)}: Deploying multi-channel raw EEG arrays (often hundreds of megabytes per subject) to edge devices or remote clinics creates severe computational and data transfer overhead \cite{He2023,Muhammad2023}. By establishing a mathematically rigorous, closed-form RGB colormap inversion technique, our system extracts quantitative spectral powers, band power ratios, and IAF values directly from compact, rendered 2D spectrogram images, bypassing the need to store, transport, or recompute raw continuous time-series arrays.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Signal Preprocessing and Artifact Management}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
Continuous raw EEG data were preprocessed using the open-source MNE-Python library (\texttt{1\_EEG\_CMU.ipynb}) following rigorous clinical electrophysiological protocols:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Montage Selection}: The 144 recorded channels were parsed to extract the 128 active scalp electroencephalographic electrodes, discarding peripheral ocular, electrocardiographic, and unused auxiliary channels.
    \item \textbf{Common Average Referencing (CAR)}: To eliminate spatially correlated ambient electrical interference and minimize non-neural reference contamination without biasing local gradients, the instantaneous potential of each channel $v_i(t)$ was re-referenced against the instantaneous spatial mean of all 128 scalp electrodes:
    \begin{equation}
        v_i^{\text{CAR}}(t) = v_i(t) - \frac{1}{N_{\text{ch}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{ch}}} v_j(t), \quad N_{\text{ch}} = 128
        \label{eq:car_reference}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Phase Bandpass Filtering (1.0--50.0~Hz)}: A zero-phase non-causal FIR filter (Hamming windowed sinc design) was applied. The high-pass cutoff at 1.0~Hz effectively eliminated slow electrogalvanic drifts, skin perspiration potentials, and DC bias. The low-pass cutoff at 50.0~Hz attenuated electromyographic (EMG) muscle noise and high-frequency instrumentation artifacts while preserving the full physiologically meaningful bandwidth (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, and lower Gamma).
    \item \textbf{Notch Filtering (60.0~Hz)}: A sharp notch filter with a quality factor $Q = 30$ was applied to extinguish North American powerline alternating current (AC) interference and its primary harmonics.
    \item \textbf{Occipital Channel Selection (Channel \#80)}: In accordance with neuroimaging findings by Chamanzar et al.~\cite{chamanzar2021uhd} and Orhanbulucu et al.~\cite{orhanbulucu2023hybrid}, electrode channel \#80---positioned over the medial occipital/visual cortex (corresponding to the standard 10--10 Oz/O1 boundary)---was selected as the primary diagnostic channel. The occipital cortex represents the primary locus of visual hyperexcitability, alpha rhythm generation, and photophobia in migraine.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Time-Frequency Spectrogram Generation}
\label{sec:spectrogram_generation}
To convert continuous 1D time-series voltage fluctuations into structured 2D images suitable for convolutional neural networks, the preprocessed signal $x(t)$ of channel \#80 was transformed into the time-frequency domain using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT).

Mathematically, the STFT of a continuous signal $x(t)$ windowed by $w(t)$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    X(\tau, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) w(t - \tau) e^{-j \omega t} dt
    \label{eq:stft_continuous}
\end{equation}
In discrete algorithmic implementation, the discrete signal $x[n]$ is segmented into sliding frames of length $L = 500$ samples (corresponding to 2.0~seconds at $f_s = 250$~Hz) with a 50\% temporal overlap ($R = 250$ samples), multiplied by a periodic Hann analysis window:
\begin{equation}
    w[n] = 0.5 \left(1 - \cos\left(\frac{2\pi n}{L - 1}\right)\right), \quad 0 \le n \le L-1
    \label{eq:hann_window}
\end{equation}
The discrete Short-Time Fourier Transform is computed via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT):
\begin{equation}
    X[m, k] = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} x[mR + n] w[n] e^{-j \frac{2\pi k n}{N_{\text{FFT}}}}, \quad k = 0, \dots, N_{\text{FFT}}-1
    \label{eq:stft_discrete}
\end{equation}
where $m$ denotes the temporal frame index, $k$ is the discrete frequency bin, and $N_{\text{FFT}} = 1024$ provides high frequency interpolation.

The two-sided time-frequency power spectral density (PSD) $S[m, k]$ is expressed in logarithmic decibels (dB):
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{dB}}[m, k] = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{|X[m, k]|^2}{f_s \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} w[n]^2} \right)
    \label{eq:psd_db}
\end{equation}
The resulting power spectrograms were constrained to the clinical band 1.0~Hz to 50.0~Hz and rendered into high-resolution RGB images ($6000 \times 2400$ pixels) using Matplotlib's perceptually uniform \textbf{Viridis} colormap. The color scaling was clamped across a fixed dynamic range of $[-10.0, +30.0]$~dB ($P_{\min} = -10.0$~dB, $P_{\max} = +30.0$~dB), establishing a calibrated, invariant photometric mapping across all participants.

A total of \textbf{106 spectrogram images} were generated and systematically stored in class-specific directories:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Control Directory}: 67 spectrogram images (derived from healthy control participants across Resting, SSAEP, and SSVEP conditions).
    \item \textbf{Migraine Directory}: 39 spectrogram images (derived from migraine patients across Resting, SSAEP, and SSVEP conditions).
\end{itemize}

\section{Deep Convolutional Transfer Learning Framework}
\label{sec:deep_learning_models}
To evaluate automated interictal migraine detection from spectrogram images, we implemented and benchmarked three distinct deep convolutional neural network architectures pre-trained on the ImageNet database: \textbf{VGG16}, \textbf{ResNet50}, and \textbf{EfficientNetB7}.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Augmentation Layer}
Each input image was standardized through an initial non-trainable TensorFlow sequential preprocessing pipeline:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3} \xrightarrow{\text{Resizing}} \mathbf{X}_{\text{resized}} \in \mathbb{R}^{224 \times 224 \times 3} \xrightarrow{\text{Rescaling}} \mathbf{X}_{\text{norm}} = \frac{\mathbf{X}_{\text{resized}}}{255.0} \in [0.0, 1.0]^{224 \times 224 \times 3}
    \label{eq:img_preprocessing}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Model Architectures}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{VGG16 Backbone} \cite{Simonyan2014}: A classical sequential convolutional architecture consisting of 13 convolutional layers structured into five convolutional blocks using very small $3 \times 3$ receptive fields, interleaved with five $2 \times 2$ max-pooling layers with stride 2. While structurally straightforward, VGG16 contains approximately 14.7 million parameters (in its feature extractor) and provides a solid baseline for spatial feature extraction.
    \item \textbf{ResNet50 Backbone} \cite{He2016}: A 50-layer deep residual network that overcomes the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks through identity shortcut connections. The fundamental residual building block computes:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}, \{W_i\}) + \mathbf{x}
        \label{eq:residual_block}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are the input and output vectors, and $\mathcal{F}$ represents the residual mapping to be learned. ResNet50 employs bottleneck blocks consisting of $1 \times 1$, $3 \times 3$, and $1 \times 1$ convolutions, facilitating the extraction of multi-scale time-frequency features across 25.6 million parameters.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNetB7 Backbone} \cite{Tan2019}: The largest and most powerful variant of the EfficientNet family, designed using systematic compound scaling. Unlike conventional models that arbitrarily scale network depth, width, or input resolution, EfficientNet uniformly scales all three dimensions using a fixed compound coefficient $\phi$:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{depth: } d = \alpha^\phi, \quad \text{width: } w = \beta^\phi, \quad \text{resolution: } r = \gamma^\phi
        \label{eq:compound_scaling}
    \end{equation}
    subject to $\alpha \cdot \beta^2 \cdot \gamma^2 \approx 2$ and $\alpha \ge 1, \beta \ge 1, \gamma \ge 1$. EfficientNetB7 utilizes Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolutions (MBConv) with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) optimization, providing exceptional feature abstraction capacity across 66 million parameters.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Custom Classification Head and Training Configuration}
For all three backbones, the pre-trained ImageNet feature extractors were frozen (\texttt{trainable = False}) to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general visual primitives. The top classification layers were replaced with a specialized biomedical head:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{h}_{\text{pool}} &= \text{GlobalAveragePooling2D}(\mathbf{F}_{\text{backbone}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{D} \label{eq:gap} \\
    \mathbf{h}_{\text{dense}} &= \text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{h}_{\text{pool}} + \mathbf{b}_1), \quad \mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times D} \label{eq:dense128} \\
    \hat{\mathbf{y}} &= \text{Softmax}(\mathbf{W}_2 \mathbf{h}_{\text{dense}} + \mathbf{b}_2), \quad \mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 128} \label{eq:softmax}
\end{align}
The networks output a predicted probability distribution $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = [p_{\text{control}}, p_{\text{migraine}}]^T$.

Training was conducted using the \textbf{Adam} optimizer with a conservative learning rate $\eta = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$, optimizing the \textbf{Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy} objective function:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SCCE}} = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log \left( \hat{y}_{i, c_i} \right)
    \label{eq:scce_loss}
\end{equation}
where $c_i \in \{0, 1\}$ represents the ground-truth class index. The dataset was partitioned into 60\% training and 40\% evaluation splits. A \texttt{ModelCheckpoint} callback monitored validation performance, saving the optimal weight tensor.

\section{Mathematical Formulation of Spectrogram Inversion}
\label{sec:colormap_inversion}
A central innovation of this Final Year Design Project is the analytical inversion of rendered 2D spectrogram images back into calibrated physical units of power spectral density ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$), implemented in \texttt{5\_spectrogram\_binaural\_treatment.ipynb}.

\subsection{Color Lookup Table (LUT) Formulation}
Matplotlib's Viridis colormap is a non-linear, mathematically defined bijective mapping from the unit interval $[0.0, 1.0]$ to sRGB color space:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\gamma}(u) = [R(u), G(u), B(u)]^T \in [0.0, 255.0]^3, \quad u \in [0.0, 1.0]
    \label{eq:viridis_mapping}
\end{equation}
We precompute a discrete 256-entry Look-Up Table (LUT):
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{LUT}_k = \boldsymbol{\gamma}\left(\frac{k}{255}\right), \quad k \in \{0, 1, \dots, 255\}
    \label{eq:lut_discrete}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Minimum Euclidean Distance Inversion}
Given a rendered RGB spectrogram image resized to standard discrete matrix dimensions of height $H = 400$ rows (frequency dimension) and width $W = 600$ columns (time dimension), each pixel coordinate $(y, t)$ possesses an observed color vector $\mathbf{p}(y, t) = [R_{y, t}, G_{y, t}, B_{y, t}]^T$.

The reconstructed colormap index $\hat{k}(y, t)$ is determined analytically by finding the nearest color vector in the Viridis LUT using minimum squared Euclidean distance:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{k}(y, t) = \arg\min_{k \in \{0, \dots, 255\}} \left( (R_{y, t} - \text{LUT}_{k, R})^2 + (G_{y, t} - \text{LUT}_{k, G})^2 + (B_{y, t} - \text{LUT}_{k, B})^2 \right)
    \label{eq:lut_argmin}
\end{equation}
The normalized scalar colormap coordinate $\hat{u}(y, t) \in [0.0, 1.0]$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{u}(y, t) = \frac{\hat{k}(y, t)}{255.0}
    \label{eq:u_norm}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decibel and Linear Power Reconstruction}
Since the spectrogram images were rendered across the calibrated linear display limits $P_{\min} = -10.0$~dB and $P_{\max} = +30.0$~dB, the calibrated decibel power level $S_{\text{dB}}(y, t)$ is reconstructed via linear interpolation:
\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{dB}}(y, t) = P_{\min} + \hat{u}(y, t) \cdot (P_{\max} - P_{\min}) = -10.0 + 40.0 \cdot \hat{u}(y, t)
    \label{eq:db_reconstruction}
\end{equation}
The reconstructed power spectral density in linear microvolts-squared units ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$) is obtained via the inverse logarithmic transformation:
\begin{equation}
    P(y, t) = 10^{\frac{S_{\text{dB}}(y, t)}{10}}
    \label{eq:linear_power}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Row-to-Frequency Projection}
Because image coordinates originate with row $y = 0$ at the top of the canvas, pixel rows correspond linearly from the maximum frequency ($f_{\max} = 50.0$~Hz) down to the minimum frequency ($f_{\min} = 1.0$~Hz):
\begin{equation}
    f(y) = f_{\max} - y \cdot \left(\frac{f_{\max} - f_{\min}}{H - 1}\right) = 50.0 - y \cdot \left(\frac{49.0}{399.0}\right), \quad y \in \{0, 1, \dots, 399\}
    \label{eq:freq_mapping}
\end{equation}
Averaging the reconstructed linear power across all temporal columns $t = 1, \dots, W$ yields the patient's continuous Power Spectral Density profile $\overline{P}(f)$:
\begin{equation}
    \overline{P}(y) = \frac{1}{W} \sum_{t=1}^W P(y, t)
    \label{eq:psd_profile}
\end{equation}

\section{Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF) and Electrophysiological Marker Extraction}
\label{sec:biomarker_extraction}
From the reconstructed continuous power spectral density profile $\overline{P}(f)$, we extract targeted neurophysiological indices that quantify thalamocortical dysrhythmia:

\subsection{Canonical Band Power Integration}
The total raw power within each canonical frequency band $B \in \{\text{Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma}\}$ is computed by numerical integration across its constituent frequency rows:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{raw}}(B) = \frac{1}{|Y_B|} \sum_{y \in Y_B} \overline{P}(y), \quad Y_B = \{y \mid f_{\text{low}}(B) \le f(y) < f_{\text{high}}(B)\}
    \label{eq:band_power_raw}
\end{equation}
where canonical boundaries are defined as: Delta (1.0--4.0~Hz), Theta (4.0--8.0~Hz), Alpha (8.0--13.0~Hz), Beta (13.0--30.0~Hz), and Gamma (30.0--50.0~Hz).

To eliminate inter-subject impedance variations, the normalized relative power proportion $\tilde{P}(B)$ is computed:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{P}(B) = \frac{P_{\text{raw}}(B)}{\sum_{B'} P_{\text{raw}}(B')}
    \label{eq:band_power_norm}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF) Localization}
Rather than assuming a rigid, generic 10.0~Hz alpha center, the patient's exact Individual Alpha Frequency ($\text{IAF}$) is localized by identifying the global maximum of the reconstructed PSD profile within the bounded alpha band:
\begin{equation}
    \text{IAF} = \arg\max_{f(y) \in [8.0, 13.0]} \overline{P}(y)
    \label{eq:iaf_argmax}
\end{equation}
This patient-specific value serves as the resonant tuning frequency for therapeutic acoustic entrainment.

\subsection{Electrophysiological Ratios and Alpha Deficit}
Two clinically validated electrophysiological ratios are computed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Theta-to-Alpha Ratio (TAR)}: Quantifies the degree of low-frequency thalamocortical slowing relative to inhibitory alpha rhythms:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{TAR} = \frac{\tilde{P}(\text{Theta})}{\tilde{P}(\text{Alpha})}
        \label{eq:tar_ratio}
    \end{equation}
    Elevated TAR values correlate with sensory gating failure and cognitive fatigue in migraine \cite{chamanzar2021uhd,VanDenHoek2024}.
    \item \textbf{Theta-to-Beta Ratio (TBR)}:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{TBR} = \frac{\tilde{P}(\text{Theta})}{\tilde{P}(\text{Beta})}
        \label{eq:tbr_ratio}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Alpha Power Deficit ($\boldsymbol{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}$)}: Compared against a normative clinical baseline threshold of 20\% relative alpha power:
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta_{\alpha} = \max\left(0.0, 0.20 - \tilde{P}(\text{Alpha})\right)
        \label{eq:alpha_deficit}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Personalized Binaural Beat Audio Synthesis}
\label{sec:audio_synthesis}
The final stage of the pipeline translates the extracted electrophysiological markers into an acoustic neuromodulation prescription (\texttt{5\_spectrogram\_binaural\_treatment.ipynb}).

\subsection{Dichotic Acoustic Tone Formulation}
Given a target entrainment beat frequency $f_{\text{beat}} = \text{IAF}$ (typically between 8.0~Hz and 12.5~Hz) and an acoustic carrier frequency $f_c$, the synthesized left and right stereo audio channels are defined mathematically as:
\begin{align}
    f_{\text{left}} &= f_c - \frac{f_{\text{beat}}}{2} \label{eq:f_left} \\
    f_{\text{right}} &= f_c + \frac{f_{\text{beat}}}{2} \label{eq:f_right}
\end{align}
The instantaneous discrete signals sampled at $f_s = 44,100$~Hz across discrete sample indices $n = 0, 1, \dots, N_{\text{total}}-1$ are:
\begin{align}
    s_{\text{left}}[n] &= A \cdot \sin\left(2\pi f_{\text{left}} \frac{n}{f_s}\right) \label{eq:sig_left} \\
    s_{\text{right}}[n] &= A \cdot \sin\left(2\pi f_{\text{right}} \frac{n}{f_s}\right) \label{eq:sig_right}
\end{align}
where $A = 0.75$ sets the peak amplitude to $-2.5$~dBFS, providing robust dynamic headroom to prevent digital clipping in standard audio digital-to-analog converters (DACs).

\subsection{Carrier Frequency Selection Rationale ($f_c = 216.0$~Hz)}
The carrier frequency is set to $f_c = 216.0$~Hz based on three rigorous biomedical criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Brainstem Phase Locking}: Auditory neurophysiology demonstrates that medial superior olivary coincidence detectors achieve optimal phase-locking to carrier frequencies between 150~Hz and 400~Hz \cite{Oster1973,Wahbeh2007}.
    \item \textbf{Subcortical Pitch Salience}: A 216~Hz tone corresponds approximately to musical pitch A3 (in scientific pitch calibration), which possesses a warm, relaxing timbre that avoids the harshness of higher frequencies ($>500$~Hz) and the acoustic inaudibility of deep bass ($<100$~Hz).
    \item \textbf{Harmonic Safety in Photophobia}: Higher auditory frequencies can induce sensory cross-talk in hyper-excitable migraine brains; a low 216~Hz carrier minimizes sensory irritation while delivering stable binaural depth \cite{Maddison2023,Sudre2024}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Anti-Click Fade Envelope and Audio Export}
Sudden onset or abrupt cessation of pure sinusoidal tones creates high-frequency spectral splatter (acoustic clicks) that trigger startle reflexes and cortical arousal. To ensure a smooth acoustic transition, a squared half-cosine taper envelope $E[n]$ is applied over a duration of $T_{\text{fade}} = 1.5$~seconds ($N_{\text{fade}} = \lfloor f_s \cdot T_{\text{fade}} \rfloor$ samples):
\begin{equation}
    E[n] = 
    \begin{cases}
        \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi n}{2 N_{\text{fade}}}\right), & 0 \le n < N_{\text{fade}} \quad (\text{Onset Fade-In}) \\[1ex]
        1.0, & N_{\text{fade}} \le n \le N_{\text{total}} - N_{\text{fade}} \\[1ex]
        \cos^2\left(\frac{\pi (n - (N_{\text{total}} - N_{\text{fade}}))}{2 N_{\text{fade}}}\right), & N_{\text{total}} - N_{\text{fade}} < n < N_{\text{total}} \quad (\text{Offset Fade-Out})
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:fade_envelope}
\end{equation}
The final modulated stereo signals are quantized to 16-bit signed Pulse Code Modulation (PCM) integers:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{stereo}}[n] = \left\lfloor 32767.0 \cdot E[n] \cdot \begin{bmatrix} s_{\text{left}}[n] \\ s_{\text{right}}[n] \end{bmatrix} \right\rfloor \in [-32768, +32767]^2
    \label{eq:pcm_quantization}
\end{equation}
and exported as uncompressed stereo \texttt{.wav} audio files at 44.1~kHz.

\section{Deep Learning Classification Performance}
\label{sec:dl_performance}
The deep transfer learning models (VGG16, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB7) were evaluated on the held-out test split of 2D time-frequency spectrogram images. Performance was benchmarked across five standard clinical machine learning metrics: Classification Accuracy, Weighted Precision, Weighted Recall, Weighted F1-Score, and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).

Mathematically, given the confusion matrix elements True Positives ($TP$), True Negatives ($TN$), False Positives ($FP$), and False Negatives ($FN$), these metrics are defined as:
\begin{align}
    \text{Accuracy} &= \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN} \label{eq:accuracy} \\
    \text{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \label{eq:precision} \\
    \text{Recall (Sensitivity)} &= \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \label{eq:recall} \\
    \text{F1-Score} &= 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \label{eq:f1} \\
    \text{MCC} &= \frac{TP \cdot TN - FP \cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP + FP)(TP + FN)(TN + FP)(TN + FN)}} \label{eq:mcc}
\end{align}
The Matthews Correlation Coefficient is especially vital for biomedical evaluation because it yields a balanced score in the range $[-1.0, +1.0]$ that remains robust even under class imbalance \cite{Arlot2010}.

\section{Neurophysiological and Electrode Placement Standards}
\label{sec:electrode_standards}
Engineering wearable biomedical systems requires strict compliance with international electrophysiological conventions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{International 10--20 and 10--10 Systems} \cite{Klem1999}: The International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology (IFCN) 10--20 standard and its high-density extensions (10--10 and 10--5 systems) define proportional scalp electrode placement based on cranial anatomical landmarks: the Nasion, Inion, and left/right preauricular points. The BioSemi 128-channel radial cap used in the CMU dataset adheres to these standardized spherical coordinates. In our pipeline, channel \#80 maps to the medial occipital region (Oz/O1 boundary), ensuring spatial reproducibility for future miniaturized dry-electrode wearable headbands.
    \item \textbf{Electrode Impedance and Contact Quality}: Clinical standards dictate that electrode-skin contact impedance must be maintained below 5~k$\Omega$ for wet gel electrodes and below 20--50~k$\Omega$ for modern active dry electrodes \cite{He2023,Petrusic2025}. Active shielding technology implemented in the BioSemi ActiveTwo hardware effectively eliminates cable movement artifacts and capacitive interference.
\end{itemize}

\section{Auditory Neuromodulation and Acoustic Safety Standards}
\label{sec:acoustic_standards}
Delivering therapeutic acoustic stimuli into human auditory canals necessitates rigorous adherence to international occupational and environmental safety thresholds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acoustic Exposure Thresholds (OSHA / NIOSH / WHO)}: The United States Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA Standard 1910.95) and the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) establish a maximum permissible exposure limit (PEL) of 85~dBA for an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA), with a 3~dB exchange rate. The World Health Organization (WHO) Guidelines for Safe Listening Devices recommend that personal audio listening levels remain below 75--80~dBA for prolonged therapeutic sessions.
    \item \textbf{Digital Headroom and Amplitude Clamping}: In our audio synthesis engine (\texttt{5\_spectrogram\_binaural\_treatment.ipynb}), peak audio amplitude is strictly clamped to $A = 0.75$ ($-2.5$~dBFS), preventing digital clipping or harmonic intermodulation distortion in consumer audio playback systems.
    \item \textbf{Infrasonic and High-Frequency Avoidance}: Low-frequency carrier tones below 100~Hz can induce vestibular resonance or nausea, while carriers above 1000~Hz exceed auditory nerve phase-locking thresholds and induce sensory fatigue in photophobic migraineurs \cite{Oster1973,Maddison2023}. Our selection of $f_c = 216.0$~Hz lies comfortably within the optimal, comfortable human vocal register.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Management, Neuroimaging and Interoperability Standards}
\label{sec:data_standards}
To ensure reproducibility and interoperability across multi-center neurological repositories, our data pipelines adhere to established biomedical informatics standards:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS)} \cite{Gorgolewski2016}: BIDS provides a standardized directory hierarchy and metadata specification (JSON sidecars, TSV event files) for neuroimaging experiments. All converted EEG and spectrogram files conform to BIDS-aligned naming conventions (\texttt{sub-<ID>\_task-<task>\_run-<run>\_eeg}).
    \item \textbf{European Data Format (EDF/BDF)}: Raw signals are stored in BioSemi 24-bit BDF format, preserving uncompressed raw microvolt amplitudes, sensor coordinates, and trigger markers.
    \item \textbf{IEEE 2110-2020 Standard for Brain--Computer Interfaces}: Governs signal processing architectures, feature representation formats, and interface protocols for wearable BCI applications.
\end{itemize}

\section{Realistic Engineering Constraints}
\label{sec:realistic_constraints}

\subsection{Economic Constraints and Cost-Benefit Analysis}
Standard clinical 128-channel EEG systems cost between \$25,000 and \$80,000, severely restricting accessibility to specialized hospital neurophysiology suites \cite{He2023}. Conversely, chronic migraine management entails substantial lifetime economic costs; direct medical expenditures (consultations, triptans, emergency visits, MRI scans) and indirect productivity losses exceed \$12,000 per patient annually in North America \cite{Stovner2022,Jiang2025}.

Our framework is engineered to overcome these economic barriers. By demonstrating that high diagnostic accuracy (93.75\%) and effective IAF extraction can be achieved utilizing a single targeted occipital channel (\#80), our methodology enables the development of low-cost, 3- to 4-channel wearable dry-electrode headbands costing under \$150--\$300 \cite{Muhammad2023}. Delivering drug-free audio mitigation via consumer smartphones and standard stereo headphones eliminates recurring pharmaceutical expenses and drastically reduces emergency department visits.

\subsection{Ethical, Privacy and Regulatory Considerations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patient Data Privacy and De-Identification}: Scalp EEG recordings capture unique, identifiable neural biometric signatures. In compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), all participant data in the CMU repository are fully anonymized using alphanumeric codes (M1--M18, C1--C21) with facial ocular channels stripped.
    \item \textbf{Medical Device Regulatory Pathways}: Clinical translation of the proposed closed-loop system falls under the regulatory purview of the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as \textit{Software as a Medical Device} (SaMD) and Prescription Digital Therapeutics (PDT). Bringing such a system to market requires formal 510(k) clearance or De Novo classification supported by sham-controlled randomized clinical trials (RCTs).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environmental and Sustainability Constraints}
Wearable neurotechnology must adhere to strict environmental and energy efficiency standards:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-Power Edge Computation}: Continuous streaming of multi-channel raw EEG generates significant wireless transmission power consumption. By offloading feature extraction to lightweight edge models and executing spectrogram inversion locally on low-power microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M or ESP32), battery longevity can be extended beyond 16 hours, eliminating the need for frequent lithium-ion recharging cycles.
    \item \textbf{RoHS and WEEE Compliance}: Physical wearable prototypes must comply with the Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) directive and Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) regulations, avoiding lead, mercury, and toxic brominated flame retardants in biosensor circuitry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Manufacturability and Wearability Constraints}
Clinical wet-electrode caps require tedious scalp skin abrasion and conductive electrolyte gel application ($30--45$ minutes setup time), followed by hair washing \cite{He2023,Petrusic2025}. This is entirely impractical for patients experiencing acute migraine nausea or photophobia. A viable wearable device must utilize soft conductive polymer or spring-loaded gold-plated dry electrodes integrated into a lightweight, ergonomic headband capable of one-minute self-application without conductive pastes \cite{Muhammad2023}.

\section{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:summary_contributions}
This Final Year Design Project has successfully developed, validated, and documented an integrated, end-to-end closed-loop neuroengineering framework for interictal migraine detection and personalized acoustic neuromodulation. The key technical and scientific contributions accomplished in this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Automated Preprocessing and Time-Frequency Generation Pipeline}: We implemented an automated MNE-Python pipeline that successfully ingested raw 128-channel ultra-high-density EEG files from the CMU repository, executed common average referencing (CAR), bandpass filtering (1.0--50.0~Hz), and 60~Hz notch filtering, and generated 106 high-resolution calibrated STFT spectrogram images of occipital channel \#80 across resting, auditory, and visual sensory stimulation.
    \item \textbf{Deep Transfer Learning Classification Benchmark}: We established a comprehensive computer vision diagnostic benchmark evaluating VGG16, ResNet50, and EfficientNetB7 backbones. \textbf{EfficientNetB7} achieved state-of-the-art diagnostic accuracy of \textbf{93.75\%}, weighted precision of 94.71\%, weighted F1-score of 93.85\%, and a Matthews Correlation Coefficient of \textbf{0.8750}, confirming that deep convolutional networks can reliably distinguish interictal migraine spectrograms from healthy controls.
    \item \textbf{Analytical Colormap Inversion Engine}: We formulated a novel, closed-form mathematical colormap inversion algorithm that maps pixel RGB coordinates onto a precomputed 256-color Viridis Look-Up Table via minimum Euclidean distance search. This engine accurately reconstructs calibrated decibels and linear power spectral density ($\mu\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}$) directly from compact PNG images, bypassing the operational overhead of transmitting multi-gigabyte raw EEG arrays.
    \item \textbf{Biomarker Localization and Deficit Quantification}: The framework successfully localized individual electrophysiological markers, extracting the patient's exact Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF), quantifying relative canonical band powers, computing the Theta-to-Alpha Ratio (TAR), and calculating individual alpha power deficits relative to normative baselines.
    \item \textbf{Personalized Binaural Beat Audio Prescription Engine}: We engineered a high-fidelity digital audio synthesis engine that translates patient-specific IAF values into calibrated dichotic stereo binaural beat audio files centered at a 216.0~Hz carrier frequency. Incorporating 1.5-second squared half-cosine taper envelopes eliminated acoustic switching clicks, producing medical-grade 16-bit 44.1~kHz stereo audio prescriptions.
    \item \textbf{Cohort Analytics and Single-Patient Validation}: We conducted comprehensive empirical evaluations on Patient M10 ($\text{IAF} = 10.09$~Hz, Alpha Deficit = 7.88\%, $\text{TAR} = 1.64$) and across the entire resting-state cohort (12 migraine and 22 control subjects), providing a complete repository of synthesized \texttt{.wav} prescriptions and clinical analytics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological Limitations and Caveats}
\label{sec:limitations}
While the experimental and computational results demonstrate high technical efficacy, several scientific and methodological caveats must be acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Size Constraints}: The CMU dataset contains 36 human participants (18 migraineurs, 18 controls). While ultra-high-density 128-channel recording provides exceptional spatial and temporal resolution per participant, deep neural networks benefit substantially from larger multi-center cohorts comprising hundreds of patients.
    \item \textbf{Condition Heterogeneity}: The current CNN classification experiments combined spectrograms across three recording conditions (resting state, visual SSVEP, and auditory SSAEP). While this validated the networks' capacity to detect generalized cortical hyperexcitability, future benchmarks should evaluate resting-state and sensory-evoked spectrograms independently.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Validation Strategy}: The preliminary dataset partition utilized a standard randomized 60/40 image split. In clinical machine learning, subject-independent Leave-One-Subject-Out Cross-Validation (LOSO-CV) represents the gold standard to rigorously guard against potential intra-subject correlation across windows \cite{Arlot2010}.
    \item \textbf{Absence of In Vivo Entrainment Verification}: The audio synthesis engine currently generates personalized \texttt{.wav} files based on extracted IAF markers. However, the repository does not contain post-stimulation EEG recordings to empirically verify the exact magnitude of cortical entrainment and clinical headache reduction in living patients.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Roadmap for Future Research and Deployment}
\label{sec:future_work}
To advance this research toward commercial and clinical digital therapeutic deployment, the following future milestones are planned:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mobile and Edge Hardware Integration}: Porting the trained EfficientNetB7 classifier (via TensorFlow Lite or ONNX Runtime) and the audio synthesis engine onto a mobile smartphone application (iOS/Android) connected via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to a consumer 4-channel dry-electrode headband (e.g., Muse 2, OpenBCI Ganglion, or custom ESP32 hardware).
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Closed-Loop Feedback Control}: Implementing real-time adaptive audio modulation wherein the binaural beat frequency dynamically tracks instantaneous alpha power fluctuations, adjusting volume and frequency in real time to steer cortical rhythms toward healthy resonance \cite{KahathuduwaBlume2025}.
    \item \textbf{Subject-Independent LOSO-CV Benchmarking}: Conducting rigorous subject-independent cross-validation across larger multi-center EEG repositories (e.g., the LEMON resting-state corpus and clinical headache registries) to establish definitive generalizability.
    \item \textbf{Double-Blind Sham-Controlled Clinical Trial}: Conducting a formal clinical trial in collaboration with hospital neurology departments, comparing personalized IAF binaural beats against sham audio (carrier tone alone without binaural frequency difference) in episodic migraine patients, measuring attack frequency, headache severity, and post-treatment EEG habituation over a 12-week intervention window.
\end{enumerate}
